\chapter{Trajectory pre-processing}
\label{ch:preproc}

The pre-processing turns each IGC file into a trajectory fit for analysis. This
chapter describes its steps in the order they run, one section per step. Some steps
delete single fixes, and the two excursion rules also split the flight; others judge the
whole flight and keep or drop it. Each step answers one question about the recording:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Reading the fixes} (Section~\ref{sec:parsing}). Each record of the file
  becomes a fix with a time, a position and two altitudes, one from the barometer and
  one from the GNSS receiver. Records that cannot be read are discarded; nothing else is
  judged.
\item \textbf{Choice of altitude channel} (Section~\ref{sec:altchannel}). The analysis
  uses the GNSS altitude, so a flight whose GNSS altitude is mostly missing or never
  moves is dropped, and in the flights kept the few fixes without it are deleted. The
  barometer is checked too: when it measures altitude, it serves as an independent
  witness of real vertical motion.
\item \textbf{Clock} (Section~\ref{sec:clock}). Time must run forward: a fix that does
  not come after every fix before it is deleted.
\item \textbf{Repeated positions} (Section~\ref{sec:repeated}). A recorder that stops
  updating repeats its last position; the copies are deleted.
\item \textbf{Fast tracks} (Section~\ref{sec:fasttracks}). A free flight spends part of
  its time climbing in thermals at a low ground speed, so a track whose typical speed is
  too high is not a free flight, and is dropped.
\item \textbf{Horizontal excursions} (Section~\ref{sec:excursions}). A fix the glider
  could not have reached at its top speed is a recorder error: it is deleted if the track
  comes back soon after, and otherwise the flight is split where the track jumps.
\item \textbf{Vertical excursions} (Section~\ref{sec:vertical}). The same rule on the
  GNSS altitude, with a top vertical speed read off the barometer.
\end{enumerate}
What comes out is a set of segments, pieces of the flight that no rule split, with a
time, a position and an altitude on every fix. Table~\ref{tab:preproc-steps} sums up the
steps and their thresholds.

\section{Reading the fixes}
\label{sec:parsing}

Each \texttt{B} record gives a fix $(t_i,\varphi_i,\lambda_i)$, with latitude and
longitude in degrees, together with the validity flag and the two altitudes
$h_i^{\mathrm{baro}}$ and $h_i^{\mathrm{GNSS}}$. A record that does not follow the
fixed layout of the format, or whose time of day or position is impossible, is
discarded. A missing or unreadable altitude,
including the zero the format uses for a missing reading~\cite{fai_igc_spec}, is marked as missing, and
the fix keeps its time and position. A fix without a GNSS altitude is deleted later, by
the altitude channel (Section~\ref{sec:altchannel}).

Time is measured in seconds from the first fix. Records carry only the time of day,
so a flight crossing UTC midnight would jump back by a day; a day is added when the
clock passes from the last hour of one day to the first hour of the next. Other
backward steps and repeated times are left to the clock rule of
Section~\ref{sec:clock}. Well-formed but implausible fixes, such as position spikes,
are kept as well: the parser discards only what cannot be read. The validity flag, which marks
fixes without a three-dimensional GNSS solution, is kept but not used at this stage.

\section{Choice of altitude channel}
\label{sec:altchannel}

Some recorders log no pressure altitude, so we use GNSS altitude for every flight,
$z=h^{\mathrm{GNSS}}$. Its vertical datum is not uniform across recorders: the IGC
specification prescribes height above the WGS84 ellipsoid~\cite{fai_igc_spec}, the
CIVL specification for hang gliders and paragliders height above the
geoid~\cite{fai_civl_s7h_2024}. A constant offset cancels in altitude increments, but
comparing absolute altitudes across flights would need a datum audit.

Let $N$ be the number of fixes read from the file, before any is deleted, and $P$ the
set of those with a GNSS altitude. The flight is kept only if
\begin{equation}
  \frac{|P|}{N}\geq\cfgAltitudeGnssPresentMin
  \qquad\text{and}\qquad
  \max_{i\in P}h_i^{\mathrm{GNSS}}-\min_{i\in P}h_i^{\mathrm{GNSS}}\geq\SI{\cfgAltitudeGnssMinRangeM}{\metre}.
  \label{eq:gnss-admission}
\end{equation}
The first condition rejects incomplete channels; the second rejects channels stuck at
one value, and flights with almost no vertical excursion. Fixes flagged as lacking a
three-dimensional solution count as present if they carry an altitude. Pressure
altitude is never substituted for a missing GNSS value. In a flight kept, the fixes
without a GNSS altitude are deleted, so every later step sees a time, a position and an
altitude on every fix; by the first condition, this deletes at most a share
$1-\cfgAltitudeGnssPresentMin$ of the fixes of a flight.

The barometer is checked with the same two conditions on $h^{\mathrm{baro}}$, with
thresholds \cfgAltitudeBaroWitnessPresentMin\ and
\SI{\cfgAltitudeBaroWitnessMinRangeM}{\metre}, and with a third: its range must agree
with the GNSS range,
\begin{equation}
  \bigl|R^{\mathrm{baro}}-R^{\mathrm{GNSS}}\bigr|
  \leq\cfgAltitudeBaroWitnessRangeTolerance\,R^{\mathrm{GNSS}},
  \label{eq:baro-witness}
\end{equation}
where $R$ is the highest minus the lowest altitude of a channel. Pressure altitude
departs from true altitude by a few percent when the temperature departs from the
standard atmosphere, so a barometer that measures altitude passes; some recorders write
a raw pressure reading or feet in this field, and fail. A flight whose barometer passes
the three conditions is a witness: its pressure altitude, measured independently of the
GNSS, serves only to choose and to check the top vertical speed of the vertical
excursion rule (Section~\ref{sec:vertical}), and never decides whether the flight is
kept.

\section{Clock}
\label{sec:clock}

A recorder can write a time equal to or earlier than the one before it. On the fixes
left by the altitude channel, in order, the clock rule keeps the first fix, and fix $i$
only if its time is later than that of every fix before it,
\begin{equation}
  t_i>\max_{j<i}t_j.
  \label{eq:clock}
\end{equation}
Of several fixes with the same second, the first is kept. A backward step is deleted,
and so is every fix after it until the clock passes the latest time already kept. The
times left are strictly increasing, so every step between consecutive fixes has a
positive duration (Section~\ref{sec:fasttracks}).

\section{Repeated positions}
\label{sec:repeated}

Some recorders repeat their last position while the clock runs, so a fix can carry the
coordinates of the one before it without being a new measurement. On the fixes left by
the clock rule, numbered again in order, fix $i$ is deleted if
\begin{equation}
  \varphi_i=\varphi_{i-1}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\lambda_i=\lambda_{i-1}.
  \label{eq:repeated}
\end{equation}
The comparison is exact: the two records carry the same digits. Altitudes are not
compared. In a run of fixes at the same position only the first is kept.

\section{Fast tracks}
\label{sec:fasttracks}

A free flight spends part of its time climbing in thermals at a low ground speed, so
the typical speed of its track stays well below the top speed of the wing. A track
with a higher typical speed comes from something else, such as a powered aircraft or a
file with compressed times, and the whole flight is dropped.

Let $n$ be the number of fixes left by the previous steps. Between fix $i$ and
fix $i+1$ the horizontal distance is taken on a sphere of radius
$R=\SI{6371}{\kilo\metre}$, with the haversine formula
\begin{equation}
  d_i=2R\arcsin\sqrt{\sin^2\frac{\varphi_{i+1}-\varphi_i}{2}
  +\cos\varphi_i\cos\varphi_{i+1}\sin^2\frac{\lambda_{i+1}-\lambda_i}{2}},
  \label{eq:haversine}
\end{equation}
with the angles in radians, and the step speed is
\begin{equation}
  v_i=\frac{d_i}{t_{i+1}-t_i},\qquad i=1,\dots,n-1.
  \label{eq:step-speed}
\end{equation}
The clock rule makes every denominator positive. The typical speed of the flight is the median of its step speeds,
\begin{equation}
  \tilde v=\operatorname{median}\{v_1,\dots,v_{n-1}\},
  \label{eq:median-speed}
\end{equation}
and the flight is kept if $\tilde v$ does not exceed
\SI[per-mode=symbol]{\cfgSpeedMedianMaxKmhPara}{\kilo\metre\per\hour} for
paragliders and
\SI[per-mode=symbol]{\cfgSpeedMedianMaxKmhHang}{\kilo\metre\per\hour} for hang
gliders. A flight with fewer than two fixes has no step and is kept. The rule deletes
no fix, and the median is not moved by a few wrong steps, such as a position spike:
those are left to the horizontal excursion rule (Section~\ref{sec:excursions}).

The thresholds were read off the distribution of $\tilde v$ over all flights
(Figure~\ref{fig:median-speed}). In both disciplines the bulk of the distribution ends
below the threshold, and beyond it only a sparse tail of single flights remains.
Split by wing class (Figures~\ref{fig:median-speed-para-classes}
and~\ref{fig:median-speed-hang-classes}), the bulk of every class stays below the
threshold. The faster shoulder of the hang-glider distribution comes from the rigid
Class~2 wings.

\section{Horizontal excursions}
\label{sec:excursions}

A step whose speed $v_i$, Equation~\eqref{eq:step-speed}, exceeds what any glider of the
discipline can fly is a recorder error, but the step alone does not say what went wrong:
the track may leave its path and come back, as in a spike, or go on from the new
position, as in a jump. The excursion rule tells the two apart by looking ahead. It has
two parameters: the top speed $v_{\max}$, read off the data, and a time $W$, set by hand.

The rule scans the fixes left by the previous steps in order, against an anchor $a$, the
last fix accepted. Fix $j$ is reachable from the anchor if
\begin{equation}
  d(a,j)\leq v_{\max}\,(t_j-t_a),
  \label{eq:reachable}
\end{equation}
with $d(a,j)$ the distance of Equation~\eqref{eq:haversine}. A reachable fix is accepted
and becomes the anchor. An unreachable fix $j$ opens a block, and the rule looks for a
later fix $k$ reachable from the anchor with $t_k-t_j\leq W=\SI{\cfgExcursionWindowS}{\second}$.
If there is one, the track came back: fixes $j$ to $k-1$ are deleted and $k$ becomes the
anchor. If there is none, the flight is split before $j$ and a new segment starts from
$j$ (Figure~\ref{fig:excursion-rule}). A block still open at the end of the track is
deleted. The splits bound the segments of the flight, inside which the vertical rule runs
(Section~\ref{sec:vertical}).

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{generated/figures/preprocessing/excursion-rule}
\caption{The excursion rule on two synthetic tracks along one coordinate, one fix per
second, with a top speed lowered for legibility. Grey dots: fixes kept; blue dot: the

(a) Three fixes leave the cone and the next comes back within $W$: the three are deleted

$W=\SI{\cfgExcursionWindowS}{\second}$ of the opening of the block (grey band), so the

\label{fig:excursion-rule}
\end{figure}

The first anchor of each segment, at the start of the track or after a split, is the first
fix whose step to the next satisfies Equation~\eqref{eq:reachable}, and the fixes before
it are deleted. An isolated wrong fix disagrees with both its neighbours, so it never
becomes an anchor, which would make the good fixes after it unreachable. The price is
that a good fix followed by a spike is deleted with it.

The top speed must lie above the speed of real flight, and the rule tells which speed
matters. Over a window of length $W$, the speed from fix $i$ is
\begin{equation}
  u_i=\frac{d(i,\ell)}{t_\ell-t_i},\qquad \ell=\min\{k:\ t_k-t_i\geq W\}.
  \label{eq:window-speed}
\end{equation}
If a stretch of real flight is faster than $v_{\max}$, its first fast step opens a block
after an anchor $a$ on the track. The window from $a$ covers real flight and, unless it
spans a gap, ends within $W$ of the opening, at a fix that is reachable if
$u_a\leq v_{\max}$. A top speed above every window speed of real flight therefore never
splits real flight: a fast stretch can lose its fixes, but the rule finds a reachable fix
in time (Figure~\ref{fig:excursion-real-flight}).

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{generated/figures/preprocessing/excursion-real-flight}
\caption{Real flight faster than the top speed, on synthetic tracks drawn as in

too, since the new segment starts at the first fix whose step to the next is reachable.}
\label{fig:excursion-real-flight}
\end{figure}

Computed from every fix, the window speed also follows real flight much further than the
step speed: a fix displaced by a distance $D$ changes $u_i$ by at most $D/W$, against
$D/\Delta t$ for each of the two steps of duration $\Delta t$ that touch it. Both
distributions fall steeply (Figure~\ref{fig:top-speed}, lower row) and then turn into a
tail of errors, the step speed early, the window speed later and much lower. That tail
holds the errors that last longer than $W$, such as a jump that never comes back, and does
not depend on the wing, while real flight does: split by wing class (upper row), the
classes fall off in the order of their performance, then merge into one common tail. Real
flight therefore ends where the fastest class reaches that tail. We set $v_{\max}$ there:
\SI[per-mode=symbol]{\cfgSpeedMaxKmhPara}{\kilo\metre\per\hour} for paragliders and
\SI[per-mode=symbol]{\cfgSpeedMaxKmhHang}{\kilo\metre\per\hour} for hang gliders.

With $W$ halved and doubled, \SI{\cfgExcursionWindowShortS}{\second} and
\SI{\cfgExcursionWindowLongS}{\second}, and $v_{\max}$ unchanged
(Table~\ref{tab:excursion-windows}), the share of fixes deleted barely changes: almost
every excursion that comes back does so within the shortest window. Every excursion that
outlasts $W$ splits the flight, so the share of flights split roughly doubles when $W$ is
halved and halves when it is doubled, and stays small for every window. A longer $W$ also
bridges larger jumps: after a jump shorter than about $v_{\max}W$ the track counts as
returned, and the fixes in between are deleted. The choice affects few flights, and we
keep the middle value, which still leaves the slowest common recorders, sampling every
\SI{10}{\second} (Figure~\ref{fig:sampling-interval}), two fixes to come back.

\section{Vertical excursions}
\label{sec:vertical}

The GNSS altitude has errors of its own: it can spike or jump while the position follows
the track. The horizontal rule cannot see them, since it judges positions only, so the
same rule runs again on the altitude $z$, inside each segment the horizontal rule left:
across a split, the two altitudes belong to different pieces of the track. In analogy
with Equation~\eqref{eq:reachable}, fix $j$ is reachable from the anchor $a$ if
\begin{equation}
  |z_j-z_a|\leq v_{z,\max}\,(t_j-t_a),
  \label{eq:reachable-vertical}
\end{equation}
with $v_{z,\max}$ the top vertical speed of the discipline. Blocks, splits and first
anchors work as in Section~\ref{sec:excursions}, with the same window $W$: the reason for
keeping it, two fixes for the slowest recorders to come back, holds for any coordinate.
The new splits add to the horizontal ones, and the rule deletes whole fixes, positions
included. This is a choice: every fix left then carries a time, a position and an
altitude, as after the altitude channel (Section~\ref{sec:altchannel}), and the fixes
deleted are few (Table~\ref{tab:vertical-excursions}).

As for $v_{\max}$, a top vertical speed above every vertical speed of real flight over a
window of length $W$ never splits a real descent, however steep. In analogy with
Equations~\eqref{eq:step-speed} and~\eqref{eq:window-speed}, the vertical speeds of a
step and over a window are
\begin{equation}
  w_i=\frac{|z_{i+1}-z_i|}{t_{i+1}-t_i},
  \qquad
  \bar w_i=\frac{|z_\ell-z_i|}{t_\ell-t_i},
  \label{eq:vertical-speed}
\end{equation}
with $\ell$ as in Equation~\eqref{eq:window-speed}, both inside one segment. To read
$v_{\max}$ we had the wing classes; for $v_{z,\max}$ we have more, since in most flights
the barometer measures the same motion independently of the GNSS. In the flights whose
barometer is a witness (Section~\ref{sec:altchannel}), its speeds,
Equation~\eqref{eq:vertical-speed} with $h^{\mathrm{baro}}$ in place of $z$, show real
vertical motion without the errors of the GNSS (Figure~\ref{fig:vertical-speed}).

Over windows the barometer falls steeply, alike for every wing class (upper row), then
flattens into a floor. In the lower row the GNSS lies above the barometer while both fall,
since it adds its own errors, and beyond the fall the two merge into one floor: speeds no
glider sustains over $W$, shared by both channels, hence errors common to the two. Real
vertical motion therefore ends where the fall of the barometer meets the floor, and we set
$v_{z,\max}$ there: \SI[per-mode=symbol]{\cfgVerticalMaxMsPara}{\metre\per\second} for
paragliders and \SI[per-mode=symbol]{\cfgVerticalMaxMsHang}{\metre\per\second} for hang
gliders, whose fewer flights make the curves noisier and the reading coarser. The same
threshold judges the flights without a witness: the vertical speed of real flight depends
on the wing and on the air, which the recorder does not change.

The barometer also checks the threshold. On the pressure altitude of the witness flights
the rule deletes far fewer fixes and splits fewer flights than on the GNSS altitude
(Table~\ref{tab:vertical-excursions}). There it can only remove real flight or an error
the two channels share, so this bounds from above the real flight it removes from the
GNSS altitude.
